Good afternoon and good evening wishes

Mornings get all the messages. A hello in the afternoon slump or at sunset stands out because few people send one.

Good afternoon

  • Good afternoon! Half the day is done. You are doing fine. Have you eaten?

  • Good afternoon. A small break now will save the whole evening. Take it.

  • Hello from the sleepy side of the day. Good afternoon, and stay away from that third tea.

  • Good afternoon! May the second half of your day be lighter than the first.

  • Good afternoon. Lunch first, problems later.

  • Good afternoon. Step away from the screen for two minutes and drink some water. Orders.

  • Lunch done? If not, this is your reminder. Good afternoon.

  • Hope the day is going gently. Half of it is already behind you.

Good evening

  • Good evening! The work is done, or done enough. Time to be a person again.

  • Good evening. The sky is putting on a show today. Step out and look.

  • Good evening! Tea is better with company. Come over.

  • Good evening. I hope the day treated you well. If it did not, the evening will.

  • The sun is setting on a day you got through. Good evening.

  • Good evening. The day is nearly done, and you did what you could. That is enough.

  • The sky is putting on a show right now. Go to a window. Good evening.

  • Evening tea is ready at my end. Call when you are free.

The hours nobody greets

Morning and night have their rituals. The afternoon has only the slump after lunch, and the evening only the tired journey home. A message at four or at seven arrives when people most need a small lift, and it stands out because so few are sent.

Make it yours

  • Afternoon: ask about food. In India "Did you eat?" means "I care about you".
  • Afternoon at work: "Halfway there. Tea break at four?"
  • Evening: invite. A walk, a call, tea on the balcony.
  • Evening for elders: "Evening walk done? Who did you meet today?"

Who appreciates them most

People working long shifts, parents at home through quiet afternoons, students in exam weeks, and friends in other time zones, for whom your evening is their morning. Check the clock at their end before you send.

What to avoid

  • Messages during someone's known rest or prayer time.
  • Greetings to a busy work group in the middle of the working day.

Make it yours

  • An afternoon message is a good moment to ask "Did you eat?"
  • An evening message can invite: a walk, a tea, a call.
