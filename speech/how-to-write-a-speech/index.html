How to write a speech, step by step

A speech is not an essay read aloud. An essay is for the eye; a reader can go back and read a line again. A speech is for the ear; the listener hears each sentence once. That single difference decides everything about how a speech should be written. This method works for a two minute assembly talk and for a ten minute address.

Step 1: Answer three questions before you write

  • Who is listening? Small children, classmates, parents, colleagues, elders? Their age and mood decide your words and your examples.
  • How long do I have? Ask the organiser. Then plan for a little less. A speech that ends early is forgiven; one that runs over is not.
  • What is the occasion? A farewell needs warmth, a national day needs dignity, a wedding needs affection and gentle humour.

Step 2: Decide your one message

Complete this sentence: "When I finish, I want the audience to remember that ______." If you cannot fill the blank in one line, you are not ready to write. A speech with one clear message is remembered. A speech with six is forgotten by the time the tea arrives.

Step 3: Use the three-part shape

PartShare of timeWhat it does
Openingabout a tenthGreets the audience, wins attention, says what you will speak about.
Bodyabout four fifthsTwo or three points, each with a story, an example or a fact.
Closingabout a tenthRepeats the message in one line, thanks the audience, ends cleanly.

For the body, three points is the natural number. The ear likes threes: "to study, to serve and to smile". Give each point its own short paragraph.

Step 4: Open well

After the greeting, the first twenty seconds decide whether people listen. Good ways to begin: a question ("When did you last read a book that was not in your syllabus?"), a small story from your own life, a surprising fact, or a picture in words ("Think of a potter at the wheel"). Weak ways to begin: an apology ("I am not a good speaker"), a dictionary definition, or a long list of thanks.

Step 5: Write for the ear

  • Short sentences. If you run out of breath reading a sentence aloud, cut it in two.
  • Plain words. Say "use", not "utilise"; "help", not "facilitate".
  • Repeat on purpose. In writing, repetition is a fault. In speech, repeating your key line helps the listener hold on to it.
  • Speak to people. Use "you" and "we". "We all know that feeling" pulls an audience in.
  • One story beats ten adjectives. Do not say a teacher was "kind, caring and dedicated". Say what she did one rainy afternoon.

Step 6: Close strongly

Signal the end ("Let me end with..."), give the message once more, and finish with a line that lands: a wish, a promise, a call to do one small thing. Then say thank you and stop. Never end with "That is all" or "I have nothing more to say".

Step 7: Edit by reading aloud

Read the draft aloud with a timer. Mark every place where you stumble; rewrite that sentence. Cut anything the audience would not miss. Check every name, date and fact. Then make a small card with six key words, one for each paragraph, and practise from the card, not from the full page.

A checklist before you step up

  1. Can I say my one message in a single sentence?
  2. Is the speech inside the time limit when spoken slowly?
  3. Are all names, titles and dates checked?
  4. Have I removed anything that could embarrass or hurt someone?
  5. Have I practised it aloud at least three times?
