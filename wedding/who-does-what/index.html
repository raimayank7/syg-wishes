Wedding

Who does what: sharing the wedding work

An Indian wedding is run by volunteers: parents, brothers, sisters, cousins, a helpful uncle, the friend who knows a caterer. Nobody is appointed, so the same three people end up doing everything while others wait to be asked. A simple duty sheet, made a month ahead, changes that. This page shows how to make one.

Decisions and jobs are different things

First separate who decides from who does.

  • The couple decide: the kind of wedding they want, their clothes, the photographer, the music, their friends' list, and how they wish to be consulted.
  • The parents decide, together with the couple: the budget and how it is shared, the date, the venue, the family guest list and the rituals.
  • Each family decides for itself: its own functions, gifts for its own relatives, and its own guests' stay.

Write down who has the final word on each. Most wedding quarrels are two people each believing a decision was theirs.

How the two families usually share the hosting

Customs differ by community and region, and many families now simply share everything. The older pattern in many places is that the bride's family hosts the wedding ceremony and its meal, the groom's family hosts the reception or walima, and each side hosts its own pre-wedding functions. Whatever you choose, agree on it openly and early, and record it in the budget planner. A cost shared by an assumption is a quarrel waiting for a date.

The jobs that need a name beside them

Give each job to one person, not to "the cousins". A second name as back-up is wise.

Before the wedding

  • Invitations: collecting addresses and numbers, sending cards and messages, tracking replies in the guest list.
  • Vendor contact: one person per vendor (venue, caterer, decorator, photographer) who holds the receipt and the phone number.
  • Outstation guests: rooms, pick-ups and a welcome. See looking after outstation guests.
  • Shopping and tailoring: a list, a deadline and someone to collect.
  • Priest's list: the puja items, bought and packed in one labelled box.
  • Gifts: gifts and envelopes for relatives, packed and labelled by name.

On the day

  • Day manager: one calm person from each side with the timeline and every vendor's number. They answer the calls so that the parents do not have to.
  • Welcome: two or three people at the entrance, from both families, who know the elders by face.
  • Ritual helper: someone who knows the order of the ceremony, sits near the priest and fetches what is needed.
  • The envelope keeper: one trusted person who receives gifts and shagun envelopes, notes names and keeps the bag with them all evening.
  • Food watch: checks that counters open on time, that elders and the priest are served, and that the couple eat.
  • Photographs: stands beside the photographer with the family photo list and calls each group.
  • Bride's companion and groom's companion: a sister, cousin or friend each, who stays close with water, tissues, the phone and the wedding day bag.
  • Children and elders: someone to see that grandparents have seats, food and a way home.
  • Payments: one person per family who carries the envelopes for final payments and tips, already counted and labelled.
  • Closing: checks the rooms and the hall for forgotten things, collects leftover items and returns what was hired.

Making the duty sheet

  1. List every function with its date, time and place.
  2. Under each, write the jobs from the lists above that apply.
  3. Put one name and phone number beside each job. Ask the person first: a duty accepted is done better than a duty announced.
  4. Share the sheet as a message or a printed page with everyone named on it, about two weeks before.
  5. Walk through it once, on a call or at dinner, three or four days before.

Family groups on WhatsApp

One enormous group for everything becomes unreadable. A set-up that works:

  • A core group of the couple, parents and siblings for decisions.
  • A helpers' group of everyone named on the duty sheet, for the days around the wedding.
  • An announcements group for all guests, where only the admins post: dates, locations, timings and, later, photographs.

Pin the venue's map location and the timeline at the top of each.

Giving jobs well

  • Match the job to the person. The punctual cousin gets the timeline. The friendly aunt gets the welcome. The careful uncle gets the payments.
  • Give teenagers real work. They are quick, they know phones and they like to be trusted: guiding guests to seats, managing the playlist, filming the baraat.
  • Do not give the parents a job on the day. Their job is to be present, greet guests and take part in the rituals.
  • Thank people by name afterwards. A wedding leaves a family closer when the work was shared and noticed.

If you hire a planner

A wedding planner or the venue's coordinator takes over the vendor calls and the timeline. The family jobs remain: welcoming guests, the rituals, the envelopes and the elders cannot be handed to a stranger. Give the planner one contact person from each family and the duty sheet. Questions to ask before you hire one are on the vendor questions page.

Questions people ask

Who pays for what in an Indian wedding?

Customs differ by community and family. Many families now share costs or split them by function. What matters is that both families agree openly and early, and write it down.

Who should manage the wedding day?

One calm, organised person from each family who is not the bride, the groom or a parent. They hold the timeline and the vendors' numbers.

Who keeps the gift envelopes at a wedding?

One trusted person, usually a sibling or close relative, who receives them, notes the names and keeps the bag with them throughout.
