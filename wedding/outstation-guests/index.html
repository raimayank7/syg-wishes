Looking after outstation wedding guests

Guests who travel for your wedding give you two or three days of their lives and a train or air fare. How they are received is what they will remember, long after the menu is forgotten. Looking after them well is mostly a matter of information: knowing who arrives when, and making sure each guest knows where to go.

Start with one list

About six weeks before the wedding, collect these details from every outstation family, by message or phone:

  • names and number of people, with any small children or elderly members;
  • date and time of arrival, and by train, flight, bus or car;
  • train or flight number, and the station or airport;
  • date and time of departure;
  • anything they need: a ground floor room, a cot, a particular diet, a wheelchair.

Keep it in one sheet with one owner. The guest list maker holds the names and counts. Add the travel details in the notes or on a shared sheet.

Rooms

  • Count rooms early. Hotels near wedding venues fill in the season. Ask the venue which hotels its guests normally use.
  • Hotel, guest house or relatives' homes? Close family often prefer to stay together, even if crowded. Elderly guests need quiet, a western toilet and no stairs.
  • Make a room list: room number, names, and a mobile number for each room. Give copies to the hotel desk, to your guest coordinator and to the drivers.
  • Ask the hotel plain questions: check-in and check-out times, whether early check-in is possible for guests arriving on a morning train, breakfast timings, extra beds, and what is and is not included in the room price. Get the answers in writing.
  • Be clear about who pays. If the hosts are paying for rooms, say so, and say what is not covered, such as room service. If guests are booking their own, send them the hotel's name, a contact and the dates in good time.

Arrivals

  • Sort the arrival list by time. Guests arriving within the same hour at the same station can share a vehicle.
  • Send each family a message the day before: who will meet them, the driver's name and number, and where to stand.
  • For arrivals at night or before dawn, send a family member along with the driver, especially for elders and for women travelling alone.
  • Guests who drive need the map location of the hotel and of the venue, and to know where to park.

A welcome in the room

A small welcome costs little and says a great deal. It need not be a hamper. What guests actually use:

  • a one-page welcome note with the programme: each function's day, time, place and dress, and when the vehicle leaves the hotel;
  • two phone numbers to call for anything;
  • water bottles and something to eat for those who arrive hungry at odd hours;
  • for a mehndi or haldi, a line about what to wear.

A sample note, free to copy and change:

Welcome, and thank you for coming all this way for Ananya and Rohan's wedding. Mehndi: Friday, 6 pm, at the hotel lawn. Haldi: Saturday, 10 am, at our home (the bus leaves the hotel at 9:30). Wedding: Sunday, 7 pm, at Shubh Vatika (buses leave at 6:15). Breakfast is served in the hotel from 7:30 to 10. For anything at all, call Karan (98xxxxxx01) or Meera (98xxxxxx02). With love, the Sharma family.

A help desk and a group

  • One or two guest coordinators, cousins or friends, staying in the same hotel, whose only job is the guests.
  • An announcements group on WhatsApp for outstation guests, where only the coordinators post: the day's timings each morning, the bus departure, the map location.
  • A desk or a table in the hotel lobby for the first evening, with the room list and the programme.

Moving people between hotel and venue

  • Fix departure times and announce them twice. Start the first trip early for elders, who need longer.
  • Do a last-vehicle check: someone counts rooms before the final bus leaves.
  • For the return, keep one vehicle for those who wish to leave straight after dinner and another for those who stay to the end. Old people and families with children should not have to wait for the vidaai at 3 am.

Food outside the functions

Guests are with you for every meal, not only the wedding dinner. Plan breakfast on each morning, a simple lunch on the wedding day, tea in the afternoon, and something for people who arrive late at night. Home-style food between the rich function meals is always welcome. If elders or children keep early hours, ask the caterer to serve them first.

Departures

  • Sort the departure list by time, just like arrivals, and arrange vehicles the evening before.
  • Ask the hotel about late check-out for guests with evening trains.
  • Pack food for long train journeys. It is an old custom, and travellers still bless the family that remembers it.
  • Sweets or a small gift for the family at home are usually given at departure. Keep them packed and labelled by family name.
  • One of the hosts should say goodbye in person, or at least by phone, to each family.

The people guests remember

Elders judge a wedding by how they were received. See that every outstation grandparent, uncle and aunt is met by someone from the family within an hour of arriving, is seated at each function, and is asked at least once, "Did you sleep well? Do you need anything?" The parents of the couple cannot do this for everyone on the day, so give the job, by name, to their brothers and sisters. The duty sheet on who does what shows how.

Questions people ask

Do hosts pay for outstation guests' hotel rooms?

Customs differ. Many hosts pay for the stay of close family and of guests they have specially asked to travel. What matters is to tell guests clearly, in advance, what is arranged and what is not.

What should a wedding welcome note include?

The programme with the day, time, place and dress for each function, when the transport leaves, meal timings at the hotel and two phone numbers to call for help.

How early should outstation guests be informed?

Share the dates as soon as they are fixed, so that train and flight tickets can be booked, and collect travel details about six weeks before the wedding.
